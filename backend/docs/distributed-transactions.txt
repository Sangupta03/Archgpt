Distributed Transactions: 2PC & Saga Pattern

Sources: "Designing Data-Intensive Applications" (Kleppmann) ch.9, microservices.io (Chris Richardson) — "Saga pattern"

The problem:
A single database transaction (BEGIN...COMMIT) guarantees all-or-nothing across
tables in one database. But a "place order" flow in a microservices system might
need to update order-service's DB, decrement inventory-service's DB, and charge
payment-service's DB — three separate databases, no single transaction can span
all of them.

Two-Phase Commit (2PC):
A coordinator asks every participant to "prepare" (lock resources, confirm it
*can* commit, but don't commit yet). If all participants vote yes, coordinator
tells everyone to commit. If any vote no, coordinator tells everyone to abort.
Guarantees atomicity across multiple resources — either all commit or all abort.

Why 2PC is avoided in practice at scale:
Blocking: if the coordinator crashes after participants have "prepared" but
before sending commit/abort, participants are stuck holding locks indefinitely,
waiting for a coordinator that may never come back.
Latency: every participant must stay locked and waiting through two full
round-trips before any of them can proceed — slow, and doesn't scale well
across services owned by different teams with independent uptime.
Tight coupling: all participants must be available simultaneously; one slow
or down service blocks the whole transaction for everyone.

Saga pattern (the common alternative in microservices):
Break the transaction into a sequence of local transactions, each with its own
compensating action to undo it if a later step fails.
Example: (1) order-service creates order [compensate: cancel order],
(2) inventory-service reserves stock [compensate: release stock],
(3) payment-service charges card [compensate: refund].
If step 3 fails, the saga runs compensations for steps 2 and 1 in reverse —
no single distributed lock is ever held across all three.

Choreography vs Orchestration (two ways to coordinate a saga):
Choreography: each service publishes an event when its step completes, next
service reacts to that event — fully decentralized, no single point of control,
but harder to see the overall flow/state of "which step is this order on."
Orchestration: a central saga orchestrator explicitly tells each service what
to do next and tracks the saga's state — easier to reason about and debug,
but the orchestrator becomes a piece of infrastructure every saga depends on.

Key trade-off vs 2PC:
Sagas give up strict atomicity (there IS a window where inventory is reserved
but payment hasn't happened yet — visible partial state) in exchange for no
blocking locks held across services and independent service availability.
This is why sagas are preferred in practice: partial visibility for a short
window is an acceptable trade for not letting one service's outage freeze
every other service's transactions.

Real-world example:
Booking a flight + hotel as one "trip" transaction: reserve flight seat,
reserve hotel room, charge card. If the hotel is sold out by the time that
step runs, the saga triggers a compensating action to release the flight
seat that was already reserved — the user never ends up charged for a trip
that couldn't be fully booked, without ever holding a lock across the
airline's and hotel's separate systems.
